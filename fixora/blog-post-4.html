How to Descale Your Geyser
(And Why Skipping It Costs You Money)

July 10, 2026 5 min read Maintenance

Every time your water heater runs, dissolved minerals in hard water settle out and form a sediment layer at the bottom of the tank. Half an inch of that crust acts like insulation between the heating element and the water — forcing your geyser to run longer, work harder, and die younger. Here's how to fight back.

What Sediment Actually Costs You

The numbers from our service records are stark:

  • 15–25% higher electricity use with moderate sediment build-up — the element cycles longer to reach temperature.
  • Popping and rumbling noises as water bubbles trapped under the sediment layer superheat and escape.
  • Element failure 2–3× sooner — elements jacketed in scale overheat and burn out.
  • Tank corrosion acceleration — sediment holds moisture against the steel lining, feeding rust.
  • Less hot water — sediment displaces usable tank volume; a "50L" geyser with heavy scaling behaves like a 40L one.

The Warning Signs

Descale when you notice any of these: rumbling or popping during heating, hot water running out faster than it used to, cloudy or rusty-tinted hot water, or noticeably longer heat-up times. Annual descaling is smart in hard-water areas; every two years suffices elsewhere.

DIY Flush: The Gentle Version

A basic flush removes loose sediment and is genuinely safe for most homeowners:

What you need:
  • A garden hose long enough to reach a drain or bucket
  • Gloves — water may still be hot
  • 30–45 minutes
Steps:
  1. Cut the power. Switch off the geyser's circuit breaker — never skip this on an electric unit.
  2. Let it cool. Wait several hours, or run hot taps until output cools.
  3. Shut off cold inlet. Close the inlet valve so the tank stops refilling.
  4. Open a hot tap. A nearby faucet prevents vacuum lock while draining.
  5. Attach hose to drain valve. Run it to a floor drain and open the valve.
  6. Flush until clear. Let several tank-volumes of water pass. Briefly opening the cold inlet stirs stubborn sediment — then drain again.
  7. Close up and refill. Close drain valve, reopen cold inlet, wait for a steady stream at the open hot tap, restore power.

Safety note: if the drain valve is plastic and hasn't opened in years, don't force it — old valves snap. A stuck valve is your cue to hand this job to a professional.

When Chemical Descaling Is Needed

Hard, compacted scale doesn't flush out — it needs dissolution. Professionals circulate a food-grade descaling solution (typically citric or sulfamic acid based) through the tank for a few hours, then neutralise and flush thoroughly. This is where we'd draw the DIY line: incorrect chemical handling or incomplete neutralisation can damage the tank lining and void warranties.

The Anode Rod: Descale's Essential Partner

While descaling, ask about the sacrificial anode rod — a magnesium or aluminium rod that corrodes so your tank doesn't. It depletes every 2–3 years, and replacing it is the single cheapest way to double your geyser's lifespan ($55–$85 fitted). Most owners don't know it exists; now you do.
