Washing Machine Not Spinning?
7 Real Causes, Ranked by Repair Cost

August 12, 2026 6 min read Troubleshooting

The wash finishes, you open the door, and there sits your laundry — soaked, in a drum full of water. A washer that won't spin is the single most common call we get: 3,400 washing machine repairs and counting. Here's what our technicians actually find, ordered from cheapest to most expensive fix.

Before anything else: unplug the machine. Every check below that involves reaching inside starts with the power cord out of the wall.

1. An Unbalanced Load (Free)

Sometimes it's not broken — one heavy item like a bath mat or a single duvet can trip the out-of-balance sensor, and the machine refuses to spin rather than walk itself across the room. Redistribute the load and run a drain-and-spin cycle. If it spins fine, congratulations: your repair cost was zero.

2. A Worn Lid Switch or Door Lock ($45 – $89)

Top-loaders won't spin with a failed lid switch; front-loaders won't spin with a faulty door lock — the machine believes the door is open. Symptom clue: the cycle runs but the drum never turns, sometimes with a door-lock error code. This is the #1 cause we see, and it's a 30-minute fix.

3. A Clogged Drain Pump or Filter ($49 – $99)

No-spin is often actually no-drain. Washers refuse to spin while water remains inside. Coins, hair pins, socks and lint jam the pump impeller. Front-loaders have an accessible filter behind a small hatch — check yours monthly. If draining fixes the spin, you just saved yourself a service call.

4. A Stretched or Broken Drive Belt ($49 – $79)

Belt-drive machines (many Whirlpool, Maytag and older Samsung models) use a rubber belt from motor to transmission. Glazed, stretched or snapped belts mean the motor hums but the drum ignores it. Listen: a motor whine with no drum movement is classic belt. Replacement takes under an hour.

Quick self-check for belt-driven machines:
  • Unplug the machine and pull it away from the wall.
  • Remove the back panel (usually 4–6 screws).
  • Locate the rubber belt between the motor pulley and the large drum pulley.
  • Press the belt sideways — more than about an inch of flex means it's stretched.
  • Visible cracks, glazing or a snapped belt confirms it.

5. Worn Motor Coupler or Carbon Brushes ($69 – $119)

Direct-drive machines couple motor to transmission through a small plastic-and-rubber coupler that sacrifices itself to protect the gearbox. In brush-type motors, carbon brushes wear down over 5–8 years until they no longer contact the armature. Both are wear items — like brake pads for your washer — and both are routine same-day repairs.

6. Failed Motor Capacitor or Speed Sensor ($59 – $99)

The capacitor gives the motor its starting kick; the tachometer sensor tells the control board how fast the drum spins. A dead capacitor means silence at spin start; a failed tacho means erratic, aborted spins. Component testing confirms which — this is where professional diagnosis earns its fee.

7. Drum Bearing Failure ($99 – $149)

The expensive one — though far cheaper than a new machine. The telltale sign appears before the spinning stops: a jet-engine rumble during spin cycles that grows louder over months. By the time spinning fails, the bearings are usually seized. It's our longest washer repair at 2–3 hours, but on machines under 8 years old it's almost always worth it.

When to Call Us Instead

DIY makes sense for checks 1–4. Beyond that, you're dealing with electrical components, sealed transmissions or press-fitted bearing kits requiring special pullers. If your machine is under warranty, opening it yourself may void coverage — check first.
